GROW·organising committee brief · growth directions

How do we grow — adults and kids — and what will it cost?

A plain-language read of outside research (other sports, community clubs, small-island nations), put next to what our own 51 reports already tell us. It is a menu of ideas to react to, not a plan — and where the research couldn't answer us, it says so. The short version: growing the club is about community and structure, not being better at frisbee.

The one thing to hold in mind. We asked "at what size does the club pay for itself, and when can we afford to pay a coordinator or coach?" No research anywhere answers that. The outside world can give us hints on pricing and grants, but the actual size-and-money tipping points have to be worked out from our own books — nobody else has our numbers.
The big picture in one minute
  • People come back for the friendship and the coaching, not the competition. Villingili already feels like a real community hangout — that's our biggest asset. We just don't walk newcomers in properly.
  • The cure for the front-door leak (about half of first-timers don't return) is a short, friendly, coached beginner path with a free first taster — not more open pickup. Our own records point the same way: newcomers leak most from big, crowded sessions and stick best from small ones.
  • Starting a kids' programme is not just a cost — it recruits volunteers (especially parents), which is exactly the thin bench we keep worrying about.
  • We can coach kids without paid experts, using slightly-older locals as "near-peer" coaches.
  • Members can probably pay a little more, and the way to keep fees low long-term is to earn money beyond fees (grants, events, sponsors).
  • Outside grant money exists but is small and short-term — seed money to start something, never a salary.
Growing the adults — fix the leak, don't widen the tap

The problem. About half of all first-timers never come back after one visit. And of the newcomers who never become members, ~9 in 10 came just once — the periphery is almost entirely one-and-done, while the minority who stick become unusually loyal. Pouring in more newcomers doesn't help if they leak straight out.

What our own attendance data now shows Checked against our own records (not just outside research): the leak is long-standing, not a recent slip — about two-thirds of first-timers never return within 60 days, steadily, every half-year since 2024. It is worse in a crowd: a newcomer whose first game is small (≤18 people) comes back about 40% of the time; in a 35+ crowd, about 24%. Who's on the pitch matters a bit too — but less than it first looks: once you account for the fact that some months simply retain newcomers better than others, the "good onboarder" effect shrinks to a modest ~5–10 points and doesn't reliably repeat from one period to the next. So it's a hint about who to try, not a fixed list of star welcomers — and the bigger, steadier lever is structural (open pickup + crowding, no onramp). That is exactly what the beginner season below fixes: a small, capped, coached first session with a designated buddy.
What the research says works Run a short beginner-only "learn to play" season with real coaching and a free first session — so people arrive as a group, get taught, make friends, and cost is never the reason they don't show. Adults stick around for community and caring coaches, and turn up for fun and health, not to compete — so build the season around that, and around their time and family limits.
Starting with kids — a blank page, and a volunteer magnet

We run nothing for children today. That's a gap — but also our biggest fresh opportunity, and it can solve two problems at once.

Two things the research supports 1. Don't wait for qualified coaches. Train slightly-older community members as "near-peer" coaches — kids trust and follow people who are like them, and it works where expert coaches are scarce (they do need some training and oversight).

2. A kids' programme pulls in parent volunteers. Clubs that aim at youth and "sport for all" get more people volunteering — so youth isn't only a cost, it's how we grow the helping hands we're short of.

Cheap ways others started (promising leads we found but haven't fact-checked — worth a look before we lean on them):

  • A $399 school starter kit — discs, cones, bibs and a ready-made lesson plan in one box.
  • A "coach factory" (Y-Ultimate) — older kids are paid a small stipend to coach the younger ones, growing our own coaches from within.
  • A short coach course that multiplies — in Belize a 4-day course turned 33 coaches into 1,184 kids reached in four months.
The money — pricing, costs, and grants
  • Members can likely pay a bit more. In a comparable study, committed members were willing to pay about 30% above the current fee. Treat it as a hint to test here — not a promise.
  • Our two biggest costs are the two that set fees everywhere: the pitch and (if we ever hire) coaching. Competition from other activities does not drive our costs.
  • To keep member fees low, earn money elsewhere. Clubs that spread their income (grants, events, sponsors) charge lower member fees a couple of years later.
  • WFDF grants: they'll fund youth even if we're not yet a national federation — but it's max ~$1,500, for 12–15 months, and they back projects that show a lasting local result. In practice they mostly fund national federations, so becoming or affiliating as one improves our odds (and may help with the pitch).
Where these numbers come from. The "30%", the cost drivers and the diversification effect are from established German community clubs — a very different world from a high-cost Maldivian island. Use them as hints, and work out our own real numbers from our books.
Ideas the research shot down — don't chase these
Refuted by fact-checking
  • Getting members to recruit their friends is not a proven cure for drop-off.
  • Growing bigger does not automatically spread volunteers thinner — so we can't use that as a size rule.
  • Telling members "we're struggling" does not make them pay more (this was the clearest result — please don't).
  • The IOC's $15,000 grassroots grant is not a reliable funding route for us.
Questions only we can answer
  • How many committed players, coaches and paying members does each track need to pay for itself here? No research has this — we model it from our ledger.
  • Would a coached beginner season actually cut our ~50% first-visit drop-off? Only one way to find out: run a small pilot and count.
  • What will locals really pay, given the cost of living and side-hustles? Ask our ~56 paying members.
  • What income beyond fees keeps us alive after grant money ends — and should we become a national federation?
If we do one thing Our best asset (a real community feel) and our worst problem (half of first-timers never return) point at the same fix: a coached, subsidised, free-to-try beginner season. It attacks drop-off, opens the door to kids, and — through parents — grows our volunteers. And it's exactly the kind of "lasting local result" that wins WFDF seed money.

Own-data update (2026-07). The leak numbers here (chronic ~two-thirds one-and-done at 60 days; return ~40% from small sessions vs ~24% from 35+ crowds) are computed from our own attendance records, not the outside literature — a censor-robust re-analysis of the SOC-4 / CB-A cohort. The two headline leak figures use different denominators, both correct: "1 in 2" counts all first-timers (including those who became members and regulars — PB-C2); "~9 in 10" counts only newcomers who never joined the roster (SOC-4 / CB-A). The latter was re-analysed in full on the "Actual Attendees" presence records (2026-07-11): 130 of 150 (87%) played exactly once — confirming the periphery figure was never a logging artifact. The per-person "who's present" effect is real but modest and period-dependent: raw lift (up to ~+17pt) is substantially driven by when a person played, shrinks to ~5–10pt once each newcomer is adjusted for their period, and does not repeat across time windows — so it's a lead, not a ranking. A named, per-member breakdown for the committee (roster presence over time, raw-vs-period-adjusted lift, and an interactive explorer) lives in the internal readout analysis/internal/onboarder-leak.html — local-only, not published.
